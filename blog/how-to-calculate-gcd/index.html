How to Calculate GCD (Euclidean Algorithm)

By ToolForge AI · 2026-09-28 · 2 min read

The greatest common divisor (GCD) of two integers is the largest number that divides both. The Euclidean algorithm finds it quickly: divide, keep the remainder, repeat until the remainder is 0.

Example: gcd(252, 198)

252 = 1 × 198 + 54
198 = 3 × 54 + 36
54 = 1 × 36 + 18
36 = 2 × 18 + 0

The last non-zero remainder is 18, so gcd(252, 198) = 18. The LCM is 252 × 198 ÷ 18 = 2772.

GCD as a linear combination

Bézout's identity says gcd(a, b) = ax + by for some integers x and y. Working backwards: 18 = 54 − 36 = 54 − (198 − 3×54) = 4×54 − 198 = 4×(252 − 198) − 198 = 4×252 − 5×198.

Common mistakes

  • Stopping too early. The answer is the last non-zero remainder, not the first remainder you compute.
  • Swapping numbers inconsistently. At each step the divisor becomes the new dividend and the remainder becomes the new divisor.
  • Forgetting zero and negatives. gcd(a, 0) = |a|, and the sign is ignored, so gcd(−12, 18) = 6.

Where the GCD is used

Simplifying fractions is the everyday use: divide the numerator and denominator by their GCD to reach lowest terms. In number theory the extended Euclidean algorithm finds modular inverses, which underpin RSA encryption. In scheduling, the LCM, derived from the GCD, tells you when repeating cycles line up again.

Frequently asked questions

What if the GCD is 1? The numbers are coprime, meaning they share no factor other than 1. Their fraction is already in lowest terms.

Is there a faster way than listing factors? Yes. The Euclidean algorithm needs only a handful of divisions even for very large numbers, which is why computers use it.
